Product work with Claude Code

My first product built end-to-end with an AI coding agent.

I wanted to test a specific idea: that a product manager's job in an AI-native team isn't to hand a tool a prompt and accept what comes back, but to direct it with the same rigour you'd bring to a real engineering workstream.
So I built a working fire-risk-assessment remedial-action tracker, the kind of tool the building-safety industry runs on, where each finding becomes an action with an owner, a priority, and a target date, moving through a lifecycle that forms the auditable record compliance depends on.

The point wasn't the code itself, it was the delivery.
I ran it as a small work environment, not an ad-hoc AI session: versioned checkpoints, a working agreement, conventional commits, and a dated decision log that tagged every decision by type, whether I directed it, overrode the tool's output, or corrected its process. My user stories were written to a reusable structure, so a fresh agent session could pick up any one and build it with no re-explanation.

What it demonstrates: rapid prototyping of a functional tool before committing engineering time; keeping high-level direction across multiple steps; a living record of decisions rather than a static spec; and, above all, the judgement to know when to trust an AI, when to challenge it, and when a human call has to lead.

Stack: HTML, CSS, TypeScript, no framework, data in localStorage. Built with Claude Code, deployed on Firebase.

Product work as scoping and ticketing

Taking a product from "approved" to "ready for build."

The hardest, least glamorous part of product management is the gap between a green-lit idea and an engineer picking up a ticket: breaking a whole product into a logical structure of features, epics and stories, then deciding what's deliberately in and out of scope. I did exactly that for a document-management product in the UK building-safety compliance space, working from a functioning prototype and its exported data rather than a written spec.

I started with research, not decomposition. Before splitting anything, I reverse-engineered the product's data model, its entity relationships, and its boundaries from the prototype itself: which system owned which data, what this product owned versus what it only consumed from adjacent systems, and what fed off it downstream.
That boundary was the foundation, because a clean scope depends entirely on knowing where your product ends and someone else's begins. Every assumption went into a tagged decision log, so the reasoning stayed visible and I could show what I didn't know as clearly as what I did.

Only then did I decompose: the whole product into features, each into epics with a priority, a rough size, an explicit in-and-out scope, and a dependency-driven sequence. I chose one epic and detailed it to refinement-ready quality, several user stories each with acceptance criteria, business logic, UX notes, edge cases and test conditions, the level you'd take into a session with engineering and design.
I went deliberately wide-and-shallow on the overall breakdown and deep on a single epic, because the skill is judgement about where detail earns its place, not the ability to over-produce.

What it demonstrates: breaking scope into meaningful, logical units; making assumptions explicit and showing how they shape decisions; documenting requirements across functional behaviour, business logic and UX; and writing user stories an engineer can pick up and build without coming back to ask what you meant..

Stack: Notion, Google Sheets, Visio, Claude

Mahjong hand calculator

A personal tool during play of Mahjong.

Built to solve a personal problem: Doman Mahjong has dozens of scoring combinations with complex interdependencies, and tracking them during live play is mentally taxing, which is a barrier to beginners.

So I've built a real-time, client-side support tool built in React and TypeScript, helping me navigate the scoring complexity of Mahjong.
It evaluates every possible scoring hand against my current tiles, calculates how many exchanges I am from any shape, and even suggests which tile to discard when drawing a 14th.

Main challenge here was Delivery. Contrary to my other projects where it had to be well Researched before starting, Mahjong is very well documented in it's rules. Those rules however, are quite complex and required many mathematical and processing challenges.

Built iteratively across 21 sessions, with each feature scoped, researched, and formally documented in a living project brief before implementation, with end-to-end product ownership.

Stack: React 18, TypeScript, Vite, Firebase Hosting, Google Analytics

Sim City 4 Sync Region

My first official Mod for a game.

You have heard of the SimCity series, but not everyone knows that even 20 years after its release, SimCity 4 is still a popular. Possibly the best in its genre, I keep returning to it, and I have always wanted better quality-of-life tools for it.

So I built one.

The mod uses the game engine's internal commands to scan the region, then step through each city tile in turn: load it, run the simulation for a configurable number of months, save, and exit, repeating across the whole region. This resynchronises cross-border data that would otherwise only update through manual play.

The main challenge was working without documentation. I had to reverse-engineer the game's internal commands, piece together what references I could find, exchange notes with others on the modding forums, and work through several failed approaches before landing on one that was stable.
A recurring theme was discovering why the obvious method crashed, and finding a safer route the game itself would accept. The result saves a great deal of clicking and time, and enables a smoother way to play a whole region, something several users have praised.

As of September 2026, it has been viewed by more than 700 people and downloaded over 70 times.

Stack: C++ built in Visual Studio, exported into a .dll and .ini file. Hosted on both Simtropolis and Github.
