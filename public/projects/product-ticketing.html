Product: From approved to build-ready

How I took a signed-off product concept, with only a working prototype and its exported data to go on, and turned it into a structured, prioritised, build-ready backlog of features, epics, and refinement-ready stories.

Role: Product management, scoping and requirements Timeframe: 2 days Tools: Notion, Google Sheets, Visio, Claude
View it in Notion!

Overview

A product in the UK building-safety compliance space had been approved for build, but there was no written specification. The only sources of truth were a functioning prototype and a set of exported data.

My job was to close the gap between "approved" and "ready for build": to impose a clear, logical structure on the whole product and decide, explicitly, what was in and out of scope. I reverse-engineered the product from the prototype, mapped its boundaries, broke it into features, epics, and refinement-ready stories, and logged every assumption I made along the way.

For the build-it-yourself counterpart, see my Claude Code build, where I directed an AI agent to build a similar building-safety tool end to end.

The Problem

The hardest, least glamorous part of product management is the gap between a green-lit idea and an engineer picking up a ticket. Doing it well means breaking a whole product into a logical structure of features, epics, and stories, then deciding what is deliberately in and out of scope.

Two things made this harder than an ordinary breakdown. First, there was no written spec, so the requirements had to be inferred from a prototype and its data, not read from a document. Second, the domain is regulated building safety, where a regulator can demand evidence at short notice, so a product's boundaries and completeness are not academic. Getting the scope wrong is expensive.

Approach and build

Map the product before splitting it.

Before dividing anything, I reverse-engineered the product's data model, its entity relationships, and its boundaries directly from the prototype and its exported data. I worked out which system owned which data, what this product owned versus what it only consumed from adjacent systems, and what fed off it downstream. That boundary was the foundation of everything that followed, because a clean scope depends entirely on knowing where your product ends and someone else's begins. Most scope disputes are really boundary disputes in disguise.

Make every assumption visible.

With no specification, assumptions were unavoidable, so I made them a first-class part of the work rather than hiding them. Every assumption went into a tagged decision log, which kept the reasoning visible and let me show what I did not know as clearly as what I did. You can see that discipline surface directly in the backlog: again and again, an epic's out-of-scope column names an adjacent system I assumed already existed, the account setup, the notification centre, the assessment workflows, the permission model. Each epic's boundary is drawn against a stated assumption, not a guess.

Break it into features and epics.

Only then did I decompose. I split the whole product into six features, Data Ingestion, Document Model and Taxonomy, Lifecycle, Access and Sharing, Monitoring, and Audit Trail, and broke those into 21 epics. The split follows the product's own seams: how documents get in, how they are structured, how their state changes over time, who can see them, how people read the whole picture, and how change is recorded. Grouping by those seams, rather than by screen or by user, is what keeps each feature independently meaningful.

Prioritise, size, scope, and sequence.

A list of epics is not a plan, so every epic got four things: a MoSCoW priority, a rough size, an explicit in-and-out-of-scope statement, and a place in a dependency-driven order. The priorities did real work. Rather than treat contractor self-service upload as one item, I scoped a barebones version in as a Should and marked the full version an explicit Won't, so the line between this release and a later one lives on the page, not in my head. The ordering column encodes what has to exist before what: the document model and the status lifecycle are first-wave, the dashboard and external sharing come later because they read from those foundations. This is the stage that turns a breakdown into a build sequence.

Prove the depth on one epic.

I deliberately went wide-and-shallow across all 21 epics and deep on one: the Completeness engine, the logic that knows which fields are required per document type, reports the specific gap, and drives the Missing Information status. I chose it because it is the epic that turns a passive document store into an active compliance tool. Storing files is table stakes; knowing what is required, what is present, and what is missing per type is what lets the platform answer whether a building's evidence is actually complete, which is the product's whole promise. I took it to refinement-ready quality across five stories. One of them, "evaluate completeness and trigger a status recompute on save or edit," ran to a main, alternate, and error use case, seven acceptance criteria, the business rule that the lifecycle machine owns status under a strict precedence of Superseded, Expired, Missing Info, then Active, the confirmation UX for saving a soft-incomplete document, explicit test conditions, the data it touches, and what it deliberately leaves out. That is the level you would actually take into a session with engineering and design.

Outcome and impact

6 features
21 epics
1 epic to refinement-ready
5 user stories fully specified

The work was assessed against the criteria it set out to meet: how scope is broken into meaningful, logical units, how assumptions are surfaced and shape decisions, and how requirements are documented across functional behaviour, business logic, and UX. The high-level structure and the explicit assumptions were the strongest part of the response, praised for their thoroughness and clarity.

One part drew particular praise: the documentation itself. I built the deliverable as a navigable landing page rather than a loose set of files, summarising the major decisions with links through to the detail: the full list of assumptions, what each feature owns versus what it only consumes from other teams, and a flowchart for the visuals. Because the company was explicitly pro-AI, I also kept a dedicated decision log recording where I used AI to make a call and where I overrode it, so the reasoning behind each choice stayed visible and attributable.

Reflection

This was a heavy task, with only two days to deliver it and no support. Having an up-to-date brief and a working test environment to explore was a real advantage for discovery, and I spent about half the time simply understanding how the product worked, immersed in the what, why, and how.
That paid off: the response was praised for being correct on the high-level direction, on which parts mattered most to prioritise, and on what depended on other teams.

Given the same task again, I would write fewer stories but take them deeper, with tighter, more testable acceptance criteria, and I would bring engineering in earlier to pressure-test them before calling them refinement-ready.
That said, I still think going wide on the structure and deep on only one epic was the right trade-off under a two-day limit: a shallow story is cheap to deepen later, whereas a wrong high-level breakdown is expensive to unpick once a team has built on it.
